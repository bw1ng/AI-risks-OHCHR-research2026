1.1 · Executive Summary

The Situational Challenge

CORE PROBLEM

The Governance Gap is Now the Central Policy Problem

Artificial intelligence and emerging technologies are transforming the character of harm at a speed and scale that has outpaced the international community's capacity to govern them. International and national regulation is moving, but unevenly — with systematic carve-outs for precisely the AI applications most likely to cause severe humanitarian harm.

The question is no longer whether AI should be regulated, but how states can be advised to do so in a manner consistent with international human rights law (IHRL), international humanitarian law (IHL), and humanitarian protection principles — particularly in settings where institutional accountability structures are absent or under stress.

RISK LANDSCAPE

Seven Major Risk Categories

AI in military and security decision-making — already operational, not speculative — creates structural accountability gaps. Misinformation, industrialised by generative AI, threatens evidentiary infrastructure. Platform surveillance capitalism enables targeted repression. Technology-facilitated gender-based violence silences defenders and journalists. Biometric surveillance creates irreversible risks. Cyberattacks threaten humanitarian systems. The quantum computing horizon poses present operational risk.

REGULATORY LANDSCAPE

Three Paradigms in Competition

The European rights-based model, the US innovation-first deregulatory posture, and China's state-directed content control framework are not converging. Major binding instrument excludes military and national security applications. Global South states are systematically underrepresented. The UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance remains the only universal forum where Global South states participate on equal footing.

OHCHR'S ROLE

A Specific and Irreplaceable Comparative Advantage

No other actor combines the horizontal human rights mandate, universal governmental access through UPR and treaty body mechanisms, the political neutrality that allows engagement across geopolitical fault lines, and the capacity to activate legally grounded state accountability. OHCHR is the connective tissue between hard law and rights-compliant implementation.

UN COORDINATION

The UN System Convener Function

No single UN agency covers the full spectrum of AI-related human rights risks. OHCHR is the only body with the mandate and standing to convene UNESCO, UNFPA, UNDP, ITU, ICRC, and UN Women around a shared normative framework — ensuring human rights standards are embedded in every agency's AI-related work rather than treated as a sectoral afterthought.

FIVE PRIORITY ACTIONS

OHCHR's Most Urgent Priorities

First, advise states on rights-compliant AI governance architecture anchored in the Council of Europe Framework Convention, the UNGPs, and A/HRC/59/53. Second, close the national security and defence carve-out gap by operationalising IHL and IHRL for AI in conflict and security settings. Third, use procurement as the primary implementation lever, deploying OHCHR's A/HRC/59/53 report as the operational reference and pressing states to adopt FRIAs. Fourth, protect evidence integrity, civic space, and defenders — including by seeking DSA trusted flagger status and migrating OHCHR's own systems to post-quantum cryptographic standards. Fifth, anchor the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance as OHCHR's primary multilateral entry point.

1.2 · The Threefold Situational Challenge

How AI Changes the Nature of Harm

OHCHR and ICRC materials converge on four features of AI-enabled harm that are particularly dangerous in fragile and conflict-affected environments: opacity, over-reliance on technical outputs, weak remedy, and compressed human judgment. These features compound each other in precisely the environments where regulatory oversight is most absent and the consequences of error are most severe³.

PILLAR 01

AI Changes the Nature of Harm

AI increases the speed, scale, reach, and deniability of harmful operations — while compressing the deliberative time that IHL and IHRL require for legal assessment. AI is already embedded in intelligence fusion, target development, threat scoring, battle-management systems, and operational planning. These are not speculative future applications; they are operational today.

PILLAR 02

Direct & Indirect Risks to the Sector

The humanitarian sector faces both direct risks — attacks on civilians, digital surveillance, AI-enabled targeting, cyberattacks on civilian infrastructure — and indirect risks: collapse of trusted evidence, erosion of civic space, and silencing of defenders and journalists on whose work OHCHR depends.

PILLAR 03

The Governance Gap

International and national regulation is moving, but unevenly. Nearly every significant binding instrument excludes military and national security applications. Cross-border remedy is largely unaddressed. The pace of AI capability development is outrunning governance across all tracks simultaneously.

1.3 · Three Competing Regulatory Paradigms

The Global Regulatory Landscape

Three structurally different regulatory paradigms are now in global competition, with a UN-centred multilateral track attempting to anchor a universal, human-rights-based baseline across all of them. Detailed regulatory analysis is available in Section 3 — Regulatory Framework.

🇪🇺

European Model

Rights-Based · Risk-Tiered

The most advanced rights-anchored technology governance architecture. Twin anchors: EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) and the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI — the first-ever binding international AI treaty.

🇺🇸

US Model

Innovation-First · Deregulatory

No comprehensive federal AI legislation. Since the revocation of EO 14110 and replacement with EO 14179 in January 2025, federal posture is explicitly oriented toward removing regulatory barriers. EO 14365 (December 2025) directed an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state-level laws on preemption grounds.

🇨🇳

Chinese Model

State-Directed · Content Control

The world's first binding generative AI-specific regulation. Layered framework: Algorithm Recommendation (2022), Deep Synthesis (2023), Generative AI Interim Measures (2023). Fastest legislative response of any major jurisdiction — but content-control architecture creates direct ICCPR tensions on freedom of expression.

1.4 · The UN Multilateral Track

Universal Human-Rights-Anchored Baseline

Running across all three paradigms is a UN-centred multilateral track attempting to establish a universal, human-rights-anchored baseline. The Global Digital Compact, adopted on 22 September 2024 as Annex II of the Pact for the Future, is the first comprehensive global framework for digital cooperation and AI governance grounded in human rights and international law. It established the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance — both operationalised under A/RES/79/325 (August 2025).

The Global Dialogue's first session is scheduled for 6–7 July 2026 in Geneva — the first truly universal intergovernmental forum on AI governance, with over 100 countries including from the Global South and Least Developed Countries able to participate on equal footing. Key UNGA resolutions include A/RES/78/265 (March 2024) on AI rights protection, A/RES/78/241 (December 2023) on lethal autonomous weapons, and A/RES/79/239 (December 2024) — the first-ever resolution on AI in the military domain beyond LAWS, affirming the applicability of international law throughout the AI lifecycle.

Regional Dynamics & the Global South Participation Gap

Regional governance architectures are emerging but face severe implementation constraints. The African Union Continental AI Strategy (2024) offers a development-focused, rights-grounded framework rooted in the Ubuntu ethos — but Africa hosts less than 1% of global data centres and receives approximately 3% of global AI talent. The ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics (2024) establishes a voluntary seven-principle framework. The Santiago Declaration (2023) anchors regional cooperation in Latin America. A structural participation gap persists: Bletchley and Seoul AI safety summits were Western-led; the G7 Hiroshima Process excludes the Global South by design; the Council of Europe Convention was negotiated primarily among European states.

1.5 · Key Structural Tensions

Five Governance Gaps Defining the Landscape

TENSION 01
Regulatory Fragmentation Without Convergence

The three paradigms are not converging. People affected by the same AI system may have radically different access to information, contestability, and remedy depending on jurisdictional rules. Humanitarian actors operating across multiple jurisdictions inherit the weakest common denominator.

TENSION 02
National Security & Defence Carve-Outs

Nearly every significant instrument — the Council of Europe Convention, the EU AI Act, most national laws, and the ASEAN Guide — excludes national security, defence, and military applications from scope. Precisely the AI uses most likely to cause severe humanitarian harm are the least governed.

TENSION 03
The Voluntary Commitments Gap

Most binding instruments defer substantive rules to codes of practice and voluntary commitments — most visibly in the EU AI Act's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and the Frontier AI Safety Commitments. These are not binding on any state and their enforceability depends entirely on firms' continued willingness.

TENSION 04
Gendered & Civic-Space Harms Under-Regulated

No horizontal instrument reviewed addresses AI-enabled gender-based online violence comprehensively beyond general non-discrimination language. The Joint Declarations on AI and Freedom of Expression (May and October 2025) are the most directly relevant soft-law instruments addressing these harms.

TENSION 05
The Quantum Computing Horizon

Quantum computing represents the next transformative disruption to the AI governance landscape. The harvest-now-decrypt-later threat means sensitive humanitarian communications, source identities, and evidence chains are at risk of future decryption. NIST released the first three post-quantum cryptography standards (FIPS 203, 204, 205) in August 2024.

TENSION 06
Capability Outpaces Governance

Stanford HAI's 2026 AI Index confirms documented AI incidents rose to 362 in 2025 (from 233 in 2024). Hallucination rates across 26 top models ranged from 22% to 94%. 47 countries have introduced AI legislation, but only a fraction have established enforcement mechanisms — and only 31% of US residents trust their government to regulate AI.

Section 03

Regulatory Landscape & Initiatives on Tech Governance & Ethics

The global regulatory landscape for AI and emerging technology has expanded dramatically since 2022, but it remains structurally fragmented and unevenly enforced. Three structural observations frame what follows: capability is outrunning governance (documented AI incidents rose to 362 in 2025); trust in governments to regulate AI is low and declining (only 31% of US residents trust theirs to do so); and the binding-rules vs. enforcement gap is widening — 47 countries have introduced AI-specific legislation, but only a fraction have established enforcement mechanisms.

Section 04

Potential Actions and Roles of OHCHR

In a crowded field where UNESCO governs information integrity, UNFPA addresses gender-based digital violence, ITU sets technical standards, UNDP delivers governance programmes, ICRC operationalises IHL, and civil society monitors across every risk domain — why OHCHR? The answer rests on five structural features that no other actor combines in the same way, and on a coordinating role that no other body can play.

4.1 · OHCHR's Unique Value-Add

Five Structural Features No Other Actor Combines

Click any feature for the full reasoning and source citations.

1

Universal Horizontal HR Mandate

The only UN body whose entire mandate is the universal application of IHRL. Horizontal and non-derogable across every state, technology, sector, and conflict setting.

2

Connective Tissue Between Hard Law & Implementation

The actor capable of translating rights-compliant implementation into practical guidance — particularly in fragile, conflict-affected, and low-capacity settings every framework excludes.

3

Universal Governmental Access

Through the UPR, treaty bodies, Special Procedures, country offices, and the HRC — direct, recurring, institutionally structured access to virtually every government.

4

Political Neutrality Across Paradigms

The only actor positioned to offer rights-based guidance not perceived as advocacy for any one regulatory paradigm — anchored in the UDHR and core treaties, not in any state's preferences.

5

Treaty-Body Accountability Pathway

Translates AI-related harms into the UN human rights accountability system — framing them as treaty-based obligations under the ICCPR, ICESCR, CEDAW, CRC, rather than ethical or technical concerns.

4.2 · Convener & Coordinator of the UN System

OHCHR's Most Powerful and Least Exercised Role

The UN system's response to AI risks is fragmented by mandate and institutional culture. Each agency does excellent work within its domain. What is missing is a cross-cutting human rights architecture that stitches these efforts into a coherent whole — ensuring human rights standards are embedded in every agency's AI work, not added as an afterthought. OHCHR is the only UN body with the mandate, normative authority, and institutional standing to play this coordinating role.

MECHANISM 01

UN System-Wide Action Plan (UN-SWAP)

Existing institutional framework within which OHCHR can press all UN agencies to integrate human rights — including AI-specific obligations — into programmes, procurement, and technology use. OHCHR can develop an AI-specific annex requiring agencies to conduct FRIAs before procuring or deploying AI systems.

MECHANISM 02

Human Rights Mainstreaming Mechanism (HRM²)

Coordinates human rights capacity across the UN development system since 2019. Leverage to develop joint AI guidance with UNDP, UNFPA, UN Women, UNICEF, and ITU. OHCHR's FRIA methodology could become the shared standard for all UN agencies' AI-enabled programming.

MECHANISM 03

Cross-Agency Joint Outputs

Modelled on the Joint Declarations on AI and Freedom of Expression. Extend to cover TFGBV (with UNFPA, UN Women), election integrity (UNDP), biometric AI in humanitarian settings (ICRC, UNHCR), and digital inclusion (UNICEF, ITU). Combined normative weight of co-signatories.
